Designing memory that works like a friend.

AI assistants have gotten good at remembering us, and the best ones now let old details fade and update what's changed. But they still decide what matters on their own, out of sight.

A misread detail can quietly become a "fact" about you, sensitive moments are remembered all or not at all, and checking any of it means leaving the conversation for a settings page.

With a friend, mistakes surface early. They say something back, you correct it on the spot, and a passing comment doesn't quietly become part of who you are to them.

I'm designing memory that works like that. You see what it's holding on to, and one tap keeps it, fixes it or lets it go, right inside the chat. I'm exploring it through relationships, where the details are personal and keep changing.

What I'm solving

1.

Misreads surface late

With a friend, mistakes surface early. They say something back, you correct it on the spot, and a passing comment doesn’t quietly become part of who you are to them.

3.

Checking means leaving the chat

To see or fix what an AI remembers, you have to leave the conversation and dig through settings. Where in-chat checks exist, they’re limited: Sources may appear below the response, but whole past chats can only be rated or deleted, not corrected line by line. Most people never go looking, so mistakes quietly stick.

5.

Saved, but sometimes not recalled

Sometimes the AI has a memory but doesn’t use it when it should. Even the memory list you can see isn’t complete: OpenAI notes that the summary will not include everything that ChatGPT remembers, so you can’t check what’s really there. And when it forgets, you can’t tell why. Was it never saved, not found, or skipped on purpose?

2.

Importance is judged silently

The AI decides on its own what’s worth remembering and how much it matters, without ever asking. A passing comment can be treated as permanent, while something you keep coming back to may never register as important. You have no say in what counts.

4.

Sensitive memory is all or nothing

Today’s controls for sensitive topics are switches rather than judgment. Claude, for example, lets you opt in by toggling on “include sensitive topics in memory”, but there’s no way to say “remember this, but only bring it up if I do.” You’re left choosing between an AI that forgets what matters and one that might blurt out something painful.

What the interface can fix

Some memory problems live in the pipeline, and the RAG demo will show those through evals. The rest are interface problems, so I designed for them.

I explored a lot of ideas while studying how AI memory works, then narrowed them down to these four.

Asking and saving sensitive memories

Most AI apps either use sensitive memories without asking or don't remember them at all. Here, when a message touches something sensitive, the AI asks "Use sensitive memories about Nick?" inside the input box while it's still thinking, before it writes anything. Yes gives a reply that draws on her history, and No gives an equally caring reply that leaves stored memories out. Saving follows the same rule: one quiet line under the reply opens a checklist where sensitive items start unticked.

Side chats and sticky notes

Quick tangents clutter the main conversation and feed memory things it shouldn't keep. Select any text, or tap a word with the sticky-note tool, to ask up to three quick questions in a small window right there. A closed side chat leaves a faint sticky note on its word. You can add it to the main chat, or continue it in a new chat that shows where it branched from. Side chats save nothing to memory unless you add them to the main chat.

Fixing a memory while chatting

Today, fixing a wrong memory means digging through a settings list, far from the moment you noticed the mistake. Here you select the sentence that got it wrong and see exactly which memories it leaned on. You can then mark a memory outdated or not relevant, edit it, forget it, or ask the AI not to bring it up again. The fix shows up right in the reply: "6 months" is struck through with "4" beside it, and Nick becomes Nate.

Long-press design (something extra)

Selecting text on a phone is fiddly, and it's the gesture every idea above depends on. Here a long-press picks a word, and each tap after that grows the selection: word, sentence, paragraph, then the paragraph above. Handles fine-tune the edges, and a small star on the selection opens the same window from the cards above. The first time, a tip card shows how it works. Tap anywhere outside and it all lets go.

Onboarding

Problem

The app had 5 major entry friction gates and 2 of them were the biggest drop off points.

(experience first, hard parts later)

Solution

Shuffled the gates to increase user acquisition, subscription, verification and profile completion.

Details

The old onboarding hit users with a hard paywall after 4 or 5 screens, then gated profile creation and discovery behind document verification, so most users dropped off before seeing any value.

We had just started Mixpanel integration and I picked our master funnel to get the first insights. Steep drop off on 5 major points out of which 2 were crucial to go through.

When users download an app they carry some motivation, I used that momentum to make them finish their profile and explore the app a little before committing to higher friction tasks like subscription and verification. Users now had no gate on exploring potential profiles, engaging in community section, and watching exclusive content.

Approach

I studied the big 5 dating apps and researched on best onboarding processes. Turns out its context based. What works for a finance app might not work for a dating app. A spinning wheel for free credits works on a fun AI game but is misplaced on a social app.

I curated the onboarding to feel like human interaction. Naturally making users’ way up from email to basic details to preferences, using their hook for the show to reel them to new features, understanding their motivations and expectations and providing curated value.

I added small user questionnaires, creator messages, platform accolades, offers and multiple ways to explore the app to make it a dynamic experience.

AI Hosts

Problem

60% of our users came for the show, not to date. They were there for the hosts, but the hosts only existed on YouTube. Inside the app, new users landed in a dense product (dating, a live show, games and a community) with no one to show them around, and nothing in the app spoke in the show’s voice.

Solution

I pitched AI versions of the hosts, living inside the app. Ask Arlette and Ask BM are guides who answer questions regarding app navigation, relationship problems, taking things forward with a potential match, resolving conflicts and making the most of the app.

Everything about the app, the show, and relationships in general, from the beloved hosts themselves, in their voice.

AI Matchmaking

Swiping is dead.

Every dating app matches you on what you tick in a profile: age, distance, a few filters. But people are bad at describing what they want and good at revealing it when they talk. AI makes a new kind of matchmaking possible.

The hosts learn from your actual conversations, like what went wrong last time and what you keep coming back to. Then they set you up the way a friend who knows you would, and tell you why they picked that person.

Swiping can be copied overnight. A host fans already trust, who remembers what you’ve told them and has your consent to use it, can’t.

Gamification came out of the flagship feature research. Our YouTube show was a fun game show around romance, so pitching a plain dating app to that audience was the wrong call.

I prototyped match based games, exciting ways to match in the show's spirit, which also added value to each find in our smaller pool. Community poll based matching briefly became V1 games but was killed for poor performance.

I also pitched Diamonds, a system level reward currency with 50 reward ideas, but it needed all rewards ready upfront so it was put for later. The match based games launched and built a daily user habit. My goal was to remove swiping fatigue, and with their success we began planning to remove swiping altogether.

Gamification

Problem

Nothing in the app rewarded anything. Searching, filtering, matching - none of it paid off, so there was no reason to go deeper, or come back once you drifted.

Users arrive with a clock in their head: no match, no renewal, gone. And despite 60% of our base being fans of the show, the app was a generic swipe product with nothing of the show in it.

Solution

A new game show but app native, and new match based games to double down on the show’s USP. Also Diamonds - a reward layer across the whole app that works as in app currency.

Every action earns, every tier unlocks something, and a visible year-long ladder is the map for users. And because users earn by engaging, every diamond they chase is engagement we earn.

Details

Dating apps last about 5 years and most, even Tinder, Bumble and Hinge, were past their peak. We were yet another one, with users from our YouTube show arriving every episode and leaving after the free trial. With engagement dipping, the team, CEO and CTO came together to brainstorm. My research showed a rise in offline dating events, coaches, mixers and blind dates, so I presented PRDs and wireframes for blind dating, speed dating, live Pop the Balloon and a mixer. After checking feasibility with dev, we picked blind dating, then Pop the Balloon. Both massively boosted our day 3 churn users and set off a chain reaction the next month: users, engagement, match rate, messages, shares and subscriptions all rose.
